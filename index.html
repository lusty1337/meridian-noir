A perfume is a place held at one hour of one day.

MERIDIAN NOIR makes five perfumes. Each is built from material cut at one place, on one day, at one fixed minute — the hour the plant gives up the most of what it has, and the hour its shadow is shortest. That minute is the name on the bottle.

Hours are apparent solar time. Transit is 12:00 by definition; the number on the label is the minute we cut. The arc is the sun’s path on the day of the harvest, and the mark is where the cut falls on it.

How one of them is built

Cypress 12:04. Seven lines, and the last one is alcohol.

  1. Cypress absoluteCupressus sempervirens · Provence

    18.0%

    Distilled from wood cut in February, not from the needles. The needles give a cleaner, thinner smell that is gone within the hour.

  2. Cistus labdanumResin · Andalusia

    12.4%

The cut

One day at 43°17′ N, from first light to last. The flacon stands on the plate as the gnomon of a sundial, and the shadow does the reading.

  1. 05:00 First light. The stomata are shut. Nothing is leaving the plant.
  2. 06:20 The resin is still hard. Nothing will move for another two hours.
  3. 08:40 The shadow is halving every forty minutes.
  4. 10:50 Warm enough to work on the terrace. Nothing is cut yet.
  5. 12:00 Transit. The shadow is 0.49 × height and will not get shorter this year.
  6. 12:04 The cut.
  7. 12:21 Twelve kilos of wood into the still, and the door shut behind it.
  8. 15:10 The lighter fractions are already gone. What is left is the base.
  9. 17:30 The stone gives back what it took.
  10. 19:00 Everything after this hour belongs to a different formula.

What it costs to be exact

Orris. Iris pallida rhizomes are lifted in the third year and then dried for six more before they smell of anything at all. A thousand kilos of rhizome yield roughly one kilo of butter. It is the most expensive material we buy, and Orris 12:18 carries 2.9% of it, which is why that bottle costs what the other four do not.

Immortelle. Six kilos of flower, ten grams of absolute, and a four-day window in early August when the heads are open and the stems have not yet gone woody. We take the Corsican harvest whole or we take none of it.

Labdanum. One village in Andalusia still combs the resin off the fleeces of goats that have walked through the cistus. We have bought that village’s entire yield every year since 2017. There is no second supplier, and we have not looked for one.

Vetiver. Cut at two years in the ground rather than eighteen months. The extra six months is where the smoke comes from; at eighteen months the root is green and the formula reads like a different perfume.

We distil on site. Nothing travels as raw material — the still goes to the field, and the first fraction is in glass within the hour.

The index is not sold in stores.

Each formula is bottled twice a year, in the week after its harvest. Sixty millilitres, a hundred, and a nine-millilitre decant that exists because the hundred does not fit in hand luggage.
